## How to use this document

This is the finished copy for every Pre-Launch asset. Each tab maps to one page, one printable, or one send. Every element is labeled so it can be built directly into HTML or pasted into an email platform without rewriting.

**Labels used throughout**

- **Eyebrow:** small text above a headline.
- **Headline / Subhead:** the main and supporting lines.
- **Body:** paragraph copy. Each paragraph is its own short block on the page.
- **Bullets:** list items, one per line.
- **Button label:** the exact text on the button.
- **Microcopy:** small reassurance text near a button or form.
- **Builder note:** instructions for the build team. Never published.

**Placeholders.** `[DOMAIN]`, `[LINK]`, `[CHECKOUT LINK]`, `[GUIDE LINK]`, `[QUESTIONS LINK]` and `[UNSUBSCRIBE]` are filled in once the domain and platforms are set. No URLs have been invented.

**Reading conditions.** Lena's readers are on a phone, in the dark, often with a baby on them. Keep every paragraph short, keep the line breaks as written, and keep body text large. Do not combine paragraphs to save space.

**Global compliance disclaimer.** Every page footer, the lead magnet, and every email footer uses this exact wording:

Dr. Lena Park holds a PhD in developmental psychology and is a certified pediatric sleep consultant; she is not a medical doctor. I have a PhD in developmental psychology. My dissertation was about infant routines and parental stress.

I used that card from day one with my son, Theo, in 2018. Friends asked for it. Then friends of friends.

After years coordinating research in a university infant development lab, I certified as a pediatric sleep consultant in 2019 and opened my 1:1 practice in 2020.

One more thing, because it matters: I'm a doctor of developmental psychology, not a medical doctor. I teach routines, rhythm, and support. For anything about your baby's health, feeding, breathing, or safe sleep, your pediatrician is your person, and I'll point you there every time.

### Page 1: Your Evening Rhythm

**Headline:**
Your Evening Rhythm

**Subhead:**
Same steps. Same order. Every evening. About 20 to 40 minutes.

**Intro body:**
The steps matter less than the order. If you do them in the same sequence each night, your evening starts to feel familiar for everyone.

Each step has a "good enough" version for the nights when the full version isn't happening. Those nights count too.

**Step 1: Bath or wash**
- **Why:** It marks the line between day and evening.
- **Good enough version:** A warm washcloth over hands and face. Fresh pajamas.

**Step 2: Feed**
- **Why:** A calm, unhurried feed is a natural anchor for the evening.
- **Good enough version:** Feed however works tonight, wherever you are. The order is what counts. (Questions about feeding go to your pediatrician or lactation consultant.)

**Step 3: Dim the lights**
- **Why:** Lower light signals that the busy part of the day is over, for both of you.
- **Good enough version:** Switch off the big overhead light. One lamp is plenty.

**Step 4: One book**
- **Why:** A short, quiet moment that sounds the same every night.
- **Good enough version:** Say the same few sentences you said yesterday. "The day is done. The lights are low. Time to rest."

**Step 5: One song**
- **Why:** Your voice, repeated, becomes part of the rhythm.
- **Good enough version:** Hum the same tune. It doesn't need words, and you don't need to sing well.

**Step 6: Down**
- **Why:** The rhythm ends in the same place every night.
- **Good enough version:** Down, on their back, in a safe sleep space set up the way current AAP guidance describes (see Page 2). If tonight needs extra comfort before that, that's okay. You can always come back to the order tomorrow.

### Page 3: When the night goes sideways

**Headline:**
When the night goes sideways

**Subhead:**
The Pause and Read: three steps for night wakings.

**Intro body:**
When your baby wakes, it's easy to respond to the panic instead of to your baby. The Pause and Read gives you a calm order to follow.

This is not "let them cry." It's "look before you leap."

**Step 1: Pause**
Take one slow breath before you move. Just one. It gives your own body a second to settle.

**Step 2: Read**
Look at what you're actually seeing. Ask yourself:
- Could this be hunger?
- Could this be discomfort? (A wet diaper, too warm, a burp.)
- Could my baby be moving between sleep cycles, stirring and settling on their own?

**Step 3: Respond**
Respond to what you read, not to the worry. Feed if it's hunger. Help with the discomfort. If it looks like a transition, give it a moment and stay close.

**Important box:**
If you are ever worried about your baby's breathing, color, or health, trust that worry. Contact your pediatrician or emergency services right away. The Pause and Read is for ordinary night wakings, never for a concern about your baby's health.

**A schedule is clock times.** Bath at 6:45. Down at 7:15. When the clock and the baby disagree, the evening feels like a failure.

**A rhythm is the order.** The same steps, in the same sequence, every night, roughly 20 to 40 minutes. The time can move. The order stays.

You can always do the order, even on a terrible day.

That's why the guide gives you a "good enough" version of every step. A warm washcloth instead of a bath. Humming instead of a song. On the hard nights, the order still happens. It just gets smaller.

**Try this tonight:**

Most parents find they already have the beginnings of a rhythm. It just isn't written down, so it gets renegotiated every night.

Once it's on paper, it's one less thing to decide after dark.

So we build a Night Relay.

It's a simple plan for the night, decided in daylight, so nobody negotiates at 2 a.m. It answers three questions.

**1. Who covers which stretch?**
Split the night into stretches. One person takes the first, the other takes the second. Write it down. (Feeding needs will shape this, so build it around what your baby needs and what your pediatrician recommends.)

**2. What does the handoff look like?**
Decide what gets passed on. When was the last feed? Anything to watch for? A two-line note by the bed works better than a sleepy conversation.

**3. What is the off-duty person allowed to do?**
Sleep. With earplugs, in another room if you can, and without guilt. Being off duty is part of the plan, not a break from it.

**If you're parenting on your own,** the Relay still applies. It might be a support person who takes one evening a week, or one early morning. Or it might be a plan to protect your own stretch: what you'll let go of, so you can rest when the baby rests.

**Try this tonight:**

Ask one question over dinner, before anyone is tired: "Who's on first stretch tonight?"

That's the start of a Night Relay.
