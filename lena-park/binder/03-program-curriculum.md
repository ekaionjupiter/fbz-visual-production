## 1. Program Overview

### 1.1 The promise

First 90 Nights helps first-time parents build **a calm, shared plan for their evenings and nights**, so they spend fewer of those early weeks improvising in the dark.

The promise is about the parent's experience, not the baby's sleep. Every baby is different, and the course makes no promise about how a baby will sleep, how long, or by when. What it offers: an evening in the same order most nights (Rhythm), a sleep space prepared ahead of time on AAP safe sleep basics (Room), a steady way to respond to wakings (Response), a night plan everyone understands (Relay), and permission to rest and ask for help (Recovery). "Let's make tonight smaller."

### 1.2 Who it is for

- First-time parents in the third trimester, or with a baby under about 4 months old, who want a calm plan before things fall apart.
- Couples where one partner wants to help but nobody has given them a job, and single parents who want to protect their own rest.
- Capable, prepared people who feel lost at 3 a.m. and are tired of contradictory advice.
- Families who can't get on Lena's 1:1 calendar but want the foundations she teaches in her first two sessions.

### 1.3 Who it is not for

- Families with medical concerns about their baby's sleep or breathing. They need their pediatrician.
- Parents looking for a strict sleep training protocol for an older baby.
- Anyone looking for a promise of "sleeping through the night by week X."
- Anyone seeking diagnosis, treatment, or medical advice for themselves or their baby.

### 1.5 Learning design principles

Built for tired people. Most families will watch on a phone, in the dark, with a baby on them, one hand free.

1. **Short lessons.** 5 to 12 minutes. A lesson should fit inside a contact nap.
2. **One action per lesson.** Every lesson ends with a single "Tonight, try this." "One small change at a time."
3. **Fewer decisions after dark.** Fewer choices, not more rules or more data to track.
4. **Printable.** Each signature tool lives on a one-page card for the fridge.
5. **Phone-first.** Large type, high contrast, short on-screen text. Audio makes sense without watching.
6. **Captions on by default**, plus a downloadable transcript for every lesson.
7. **Minimum effective dose.** A three-lesson express path. Everything else is optional depth, not homework.
8. **Rhythm, not schedule.** Order, never clock times. No wake-window numbers, sleep-hour targets, or feeding amounts; the answer is "follow your pediatrician's guidance."
9. **Never shaming.** "You are not doing it wrong."
10. **Slow on purpose.** Lena talks slowly. Tired people don't need fast.

### Module 1: Rhythm

**Module promise:** You'll have a simple evening order you can follow even on a bad day, written down and on the fridge.

**Learning objectives.** The parent will be able to:
1. Explain the difference between a rhythm (the order things happen in) and a schedule (clock times).
2. Write their own Evening Rhythm: same steps, same sequence, roughly 20 to 40 minutes.
3. Use a shorter version on hard nights without feeling they've broken it.

#### Lesson 1.1: Rhythm, not schedule (7 min)

- A schedule is clock times. Newborns don't read clocks. A rhythm is the order things happen in, and you can always do the order, even on a terrible day.
- When the next step is already decided, there's nothing to debate at 7 p.m. or at 2 a.m.
- Apps that track every minute often add worry. Wake-window charts can be a reference, but they aren't law. For how much your baby should be sleeping, follow your pediatrician's guidance.
- **Tonight, try this:** Notice the order your evening already happens in. Don't change anything. Just notice.
- **Printable:** None (preview of The Evening Rhythm card). **Signature line:** "Rhythm, not schedule."

#### Lesson 1.2: Writing your Evening Rhythm (9 min)

- The Evening Rhythm is a short sequence of steps, in the same order every evening, taking roughly 20 to 40 minutes.
- Lena's own index card, as an example only: bath or wash, feed, dim lights, one book, one song, down. Your steps can differ.
- Choose steps you could do on your worst night. Fewer and simpler beats ambitious.
- Write it down. A plan in your head disappears when you're exhausted; a plan on the fridge doesn't. Anyone in the house should be able to follow it without asking.
- **Tonight, try this:** Write four to six steps on The Evening Rhythm card, put it on the fridge, and do them in that order tonight, however it goes.
- **Printable:** The Evening Rhythm card (front). **Signature line:** "Let's make tonight smaller."

#### Lesson 1.3: When the evening goes sideways (7 min)

- Some evenings won't go to plan: visitors, appointments, a fussy stretch, a parent who is simply done. That's normal, not failure.
- The short evening: two or three steps you keep no matter what. Same order, fewer steps. Keeping the order matters more than completing every step.
- Starting again tomorrow is part of the rhythm, not a reset.
- **Tonight, try this:** Circle the two or three steps you'd keep on a hard night and write them on the back of the card.
- **Printable:** The Evening Rhythm card (back: "The short evening"). **Signature line:** "Good enough is the goal at 3 a.m."

#### Lesson 1.4: Making it yours, and letting it change (6 min)

- Your rhythm will change as your baby grows. Change one step at a time.
- A rhythm can be shared: one adult owns the bath or wash, another the book and song (connects to Module 4).
- Give it a week of repetition before judging it, and judge how the evening feels for you, not only how the baby sleeps. If a step reliably causes stress, drop it.
- **Tonight, try this:** Write one name next to one step. Someone owns that step now.
- **Printable:** The Evening Rhythm card ("who" column). **Signature line:** "One small change at a time."

### Module 2: Room

**Module promise:** You'll have a sleep space that's ready before you're exhausted, built on safe sleep basics from the AAP and checked with your pediatrician.

2. Make simple, deliberate choices about light, sound, and comfort, deferring to the pediatrician on temperature and anything medical.
3. Set up the room and night supplies ahead of time, so nothing needs deciding after dark.

> **Compliance:** all safe sleep content comes from current AAP guidance, attributed, with a direct "talk to your pediatrician." Lena does not add to or interpret it, and does not discuss SIDS risk beyond pointing to the AAP.

- Set the room up in daylight, not at 11 p.m. Tired brains make harder decisions, so make them earlier.
- A daily "room reset": a few minutes in daylight to put the room back the way the checklist says.
- Third-trimester parents: do this lesson now, while you have more energy.
- **Tonight, try this:** Pick a daytime moment for your room reset and write it on the checklist.
- **Printable:** The Room Checklist (Section C). **Signature line:** "Let's make tonight smaller."

### Module 3: Response

**Module promise:** You'll have a calm first step for when your baby wakes, so you respond to what's actually happening instead of to the panic.

**Learning objectives.** The parent will be able to:
1. Describe The Pause and Read in three steps: pause, read, respond.
2. Name common things a waking baby may be showing (hunger, discomfort, transitioning between sleep cycles) and how they'll look for them.
3. Explain why this isn't cry-it-out, and recognize when a concern belongs with the pediatrician.

> **Compliance:** no diagnosis, no medical warning signs in Lena's words, no feeding amounts or intervals. Any concern about breathing, feeding, illness, or something that "seems off" goes to the pediatrician.

#### Lesson 3.1: When your baby wakes (7 min)

- Babies wake at night. Waking is not a sign you've done something wrong.
- Wakings feel bigger in the dark. Exhaustion turns every sound into an emergency, and parents end up reacting to their own fear.
- "Is this normal?" and "I don't even know what I'm supposed to be doing at night" are what Lena hears most. Her answer is a method, not a rule. Any worry about health, breathing, or feeding goes to your pediatrician.
- **Tonight, try this:** Next time your baby wakes, notice your own first reaction before you move. Just notice.
- **Printable:** None. **Signature line:** "Your baby is not a problem to solve."

#### Lesson 3.2: The Pause and Read (9 min)

- **Pause:** one breath before you act. The pause is for you, so you arrive calm.
- **Read:** what are you actually seeing? Hunger? Discomfort? Moving between sleep cycles? Something else?
- **Respond:** to what you read, not to the panic. Hungry: feed. Uncomfortable: address it. Anything health-related: follow your pediatrician's guidance.
- "Look before you leap." This is not cry-it-out and not waiting a set amount of time. It's arriving at your baby with a clear head.
- **Tonight, try this:** Put The Pause and Read card on your night station and use it once tonight.
- **Printable:** The Pause and Read card. **Signature line:** "Let's make tonight smaller."

#### Lesson 3.3: Reading what you see (8 min)

- You're learning your baby, and your baby is learning the world. Reading gets easier with repetition, not with more information.
- Hunger, discomfort, and sleep-cycle transitions look different for every baby. Lena shares general examples and invites parents to describe their own baby's signals in their own words. **[CLIENT TO CONFIRM example cues, so none read as clinical criteria]**
- If what you're reading worries you, or doesn't fit anything familiar, call your pediatrician.
- **Tonight, try this:** On the back of the card, write one sentence about what hunger tends to look like for your baby.
- **Printable:** The Pause and Read card (back: "What I'm learning about my baby"). **Signature line:** "You are not doing it wrong."

#### Lesson 3.4: Feeding to sleep, "bad habits," and other 3 a.m. worries (7 min)

- "Is it bad that I'm feeding him to sleep?" "Did I create bad habits already?" Lena starts with reassurance: you haven't ruined anything. In these early months you're meeting your baby's needs. There's no pass or fail test.
- Feeding questions (how much, how often, how it's going) belong with your pediatrician and, if you have one, a lactation professional.
- One plan, followed calmly, beats ten plans read at 3 a.m. Putting the phone down is part of the plan.
- **Tonight, try this:** Write your top 3 a.m. worry on the "Save it for daylight" line instead of searching for it tonight.
- **Printable:** The Pause and Read card (back). **Signature line:** "Good enough is the goal at 3 a.m."

### Module 4: Relay

**Module promise:** You'll have a written night plan everyone in the house understands, so nights stop being negotiated in the dark.

**Learning objectives.** The parent will be able to:
1. Divide the night into stretches and assign who covers each.
2. Describe what a handoff looks like in their home.
3. Build a version of The Night Relay that fits their household, including a single-parent version with a support person or a plan to protect their own stretch.

> **Compliance:** the Relay is about who does what, not how often a baby should feed. Parents build it around their own feeding plan, as discussed with their pediatrician or a lactation professional.

#### Lesson 4.1: Everyone needs a job (6 min)

- Most partners would happily help. Nobody gave them a job. "My husband wants to help but he doesn't know how" is one of the most common things Lena hears.
- Lena's story: Daniel slept in the next room not because he didn't care, but because neither of them knew what his job was.
- When the job is clear, nobody has to ask, keep score, or argue at 2 a.m.
- **Tonight, try this:** Ask your partner or support person: "What part of the night could you own?" Listen. Don't plan yet.
- **Printable:** None. **Signature line:** "Fewer decisions after dark."

#### Lesson 4.2: Building your Night Relay (9 min)

- Divide the night into stretches and decide who covers which. Lena and Daniel's version: he took the first stretch, she took the second.
- Build the stretches around your feeding plan, not the other way around. Feeding questions go to your pediatrician or a lactation professional.
- The off-duty person has one job: sleep. With earplugs. Without guilt. Their rest is part of the plan.
- Agree on it in daylight, write it down, and put it on the fridge next to The Evening Rhythm.
- **Tonight, try this:** Fill in the stretches on the planner together and try it tonight, even imperfectly.
- **Printable:** The Night Relay planner (two-parent). **Signature line:** "Let's make tonight smaller."

#### Lesson 4.3: The handoff (6 min)

- The handoff is when one person comes on duty and the other goes off. A clear handoff protects both.
- Decide in advance where it happens, how the next person is woken, and what they need to know (for example, when the last feed was, anything unusual).
- Keep it short and kind. No debriefing at 3 a.m. Then the off-duty person really goes off duty: earplugs in, door closed if possible.
- **Tonight, try this:** Agree on your handoff sentence (for example, "Last feed was at ___, all calm, you're on.") and write it on the planner.
- **Printable:** The Night Relay planner (handoff box). **Signature line:** "You are not doing it wrong."

#### Lesson 4.4: Relay for single parents and solo nights (7 min)

- Single parents get a Relay too. It just looks different.
- With a support person: someone you trust covers a stretch on some nights, with a clear handoff.
- Without one: the plan protects your own stretch: what you prepare in daylight, what you let go of, and when you rest.
- Partnered parents have solo nights too (travel, shift work, illness). Asking for help is part of the plan, not a sign you can't manage.
- **Tonight, try this:** Write down one person you could ask to cover one stretch this week, or one thing you'll do in daylight to protect your rest tonight.
- **Printable:** The Night Relay planner (single-parent and support-person version). **Signature line:** "One small change at a time."

### Module 5: Recovery

**Module promise:** You'll take your own rest seriously, set kinder expectations for these first months, and know exactly where to turn if you or your baby need more help.

**Learning objectives.** The parent will be able to:
1. Set realistic, non-shaming expectations for themselves.
2. Use one simple way to steady themselves on a hard night.
3. Know when and whom to ask for help: the pediatrician for the baby, their own doctor for postpartum mood or their own health.

> **Compliance:** Lena does not diagnose or treat postpartum mood conditions. This module normalizes asking for help and points clearly to the parent's own doctor. Emergency and crisis wording must be sourced and approved. **[CLIENT TO SUPPLY WITH SOURCE]**

#### Lesson 5.1: Good enough is the goal at 3 a.m. (6 min)

- Many of these parents are used to being good at things. "I thought I'd be better at this" is common, and it isn't evidence of failure.
- At 3 a.m. the goal is good enough: baby safe, needs met, you getting back to rest. Lowering the bar at night is a skill, not giving up.
- Your steadiness matters more than any technique.
- **Tonight, try this:** Decide one thing you're allowed to skip this week. Write it down. Skip it.
- **Printable:** Ask for Help card (top line). **Signature line:** "Good enough is the goal at 3 a.m."

#### Lesson 5.2: Steadying yourself in the dark (6 min)

- Your nervous system is part of the night. When you're flooded, every decision is harder.
- The pause in The Pause and Read works for you too: one breath, then decide.
- Simple anchors: a familiar phrase, water at the night station, lights low, phone face-down.
- These are everyday habits, not therapy. If you can't steady yourself, that's a reason to talk to your own doctor (Lesson 5.4).
- **Tonight, try this:** Choose one phrase for the hardest moment of the night and write it at the top of your Pause and Read card.
- **Printable:** The Pause and Read card ("My phrase"). **Signature line:** "Let's make tonight smaller."

#### Lesson 5.3: Kinder expectations for the first 90 nights (6 min)

- Every baby is different. Lena never promises how or when a baby will sleep, and you don't have to hold yourself to a promise someone else made online.
- What can change: how the evening feels, whether you and your partner share a plan, whether you stop improvising.
- Measure progress by your experience: fewer decisions after dark, fewer 2 a.m. arguments, less searching in the dark.
- Returning to work soon? Revisit your Relay before that change, not after. **[CLIENT TO CONFIRM whether to expand this in v1]**
- **Tonight, try this:** Finish this sentence, out loud or on paper: "This week, things will feel better if ___."
- **Printable:** None. **Signature line:** "Your baby is not a problem to solve."

#### Lesson 5.4: When to ask for help, and who to ask (6 min)

- For your baby: anything about health, breathing, feeding, growth, or sleep that worries you goes to your pediatrician. You don't need to be sure something is wrong to call.
- For you: if you're struggling with your mood or how you're coping, talk to your own doctor. Postpartum mood is real. Lena isn't the right person to treat it, and it's okay to ask.

## 6. Quarterly Answers Video

### 6.1 What it is

One recorded video per quarter in which Lena answers questions submitted by course members. It is not a live call and not a community. It extends the course without adding a live commitment.

### 6.2 How questions are collected

1. **Form** linked from the dashboard and Lesson 5.4: optional first name or initials, optional baby age range, the question (with a length limit), and a checkbox: "I understand Lena can't answer medical questions or give individual advice in this video."
2. **Window:** open all quarter, with a cutoff before recording. **[CLIENT TO CONFIRM cutoff and recording dates]** One calm reminder email near the cutoff.
3. **Selection and privacy:** similar questions are grouped into themes and paraphrased. No names on screen; no member is identifiable.
4. **Delivery:** posted to a "Quarterly Answers" section with captions and transcript, plus a short email to members.

### 6.3 Scope boundaries

**In scope:** adapting The Evening Rhythm, The Pause and Read, or The Night Relay; making the plan work with a partner, support person, or solo; expectations and rough patches; non-medical room choices (light, preparation); clarifying any lesson.

**Out of scope, with redirect:**
- Any medical question about the baby, including safe sleep beyond restating AAP guidance: pediatrician (and the AAP).
- Feeding amounts, intervals, or difficulties: pediatrician or a lactation professional.
- How much a baby should sleep, wake windows, sleep-hour targets: "follow your pediatrician's guidance."
- Postpartum mood or parent health: the parent's own doctor.
- Individual case consultation (1:1 is a separate service) and sleep training for older babies.

**Standard redirect script (Lena's voice, draft):**

> A few of you asked questions about your baby's health. I want to be really clear and kind about this: those are questions for your pediatrician, not for me. I'm not a medical doctor, and your pediatrician knows your baby. Please call them. You are not bothering them, and you are not doing it wrong by asking.

Each Answers video opens with the standing disclaimer and closes with the pediatrician and own-doctor reminder.
